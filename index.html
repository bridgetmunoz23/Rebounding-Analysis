The Value of Every Loose Ball

A possession-by-possession investigation into how GW's crash decisions and turnovers impacted the final score across five games. This is measured by an "opportunity cost" of missing a rebound or turnover, or points the opponent scores on the immediate possession when GW fails to gain or keep possession.

Bridget Muñoz · George Washington University Men's Basketball · Sample: 5-game summary (Maine, USF, GMU, Murray State, McNeese) compared to games 1-8 of the nonconference 24-25 season. Rebounds (25-26): n=271. Turnovers (25-26): n=69.
Key findings
  • One GW player being in position (IP) for a rebound but not actively pursuing it drops defensive rebounding from 86% to 25%.
  • In defensive instances where every GW player in vicinity is boxing out, GW obtains the defensive rebound 37% of the time. However, if there is one "free chaser" not boxing out, rebounding rate rises to 85%.
  • Designated rebounders convert at about the same rate as other players (24.6% vs 23.6%). The designated role provides more consistent attendance during a missed shot, and there were fewer IP plays by the designated rebounders.

Section 1

How increasing effort impacts results

This section tracks the IP variable, which measures players who are in the vicinity to attempt a rebound or box out an opponent, but either keep their arms down, have their eyes in another direction, or are generally not making an effort. Having just one "IP" player during a missed shot nearly halves GW's usual rebounding rate. However, adding just one free chaser in the paint that is not IP doubles rebounding rate.

Section 2

Defensive rebounding climbs with bodies

Defensively, the data suggests that efficient rebounding occurs with at least 3 active engaged crashers. Sending two crashers only has a success rate of 63%, but is our second most frequent lineup at ~8/game. If we sent 3 crashers on those 8 2-crasher possessions, this would add an estimated 0.85 GW REB/Game. There was also ~1.8 OPP PPP on these failed 2 crasher possessions, and adding just one GW crasher on these plays would prevent ~3.25 opponent points/game.

Section 3

Offensive rebounding by crasher count

Offensively the story is pretty straightforward, where more rebounders implies higher payoff. 3 rebounders is the exception here, and after running the numbers it seems unrelated to the number of IP players or free chasers. The only difference I found was in number of designated rebounders. GW sends ~1 designated rebounder per lineup, not increasing when going from 1-5 offensive crashers. This means the +1 crasher is going to be any player, not specifically a designated rebounder. The three-crasher dip could be from a number of reasons, but definitely also worth noting and investigating transition ability further. Since I did not study this outright I can't definitively say the increased 3-crasher O-PPP is from transition, but from film assessment I can say it's the clearest hypothesis

Section 6

Designated rebounders vs other players

GW's four designated rebounders (Players C, F, I, and E) account for nearly half of all boards, 46% on the offensive glass and 54% on the defensive. There is an interesting split between the four players, and they fall into two main roles. Our centers, Player C and Player I, crash both ends somewhat equally. Players F and E are almost exclusively defensive rebounders, with 15 DREB and 1-2 OREB apiece. This said, they are still seen at the offensive boards, just with a much lower rebounding rate. Together, Players F and E account for nearly a third of GW's defensive rebounds but only 6% of its offensive ones. Of course there are several differences between obtaining each type of rebound, but F and E lose far more going from the defensive to the offensive than GW does as a whole, so the gap is about these two specifically, not just the difficulty of offensive boards.

Defensive rebound share

Our designated rebounders do not convert at a higher rate than other players, 24.6% versus 23.6%, but they show far more effort, with a much lower in-position rate of 14.4% versus 21.1%. The role buys frequency and effort in the paint, not a better conversion percentage.

Designated rebounders vs other players

Rebound conversion = boards secured per rebound pursuit (designated 77/313, other players 75/318). IP rate = in-position no-effort tags per pursuit (designated 45/313, other players 67/318). Lower IP is better.

Methodology & Limitations

Building the study
  • Points off a failed rebound count opponent points only on possessions where GW did not secure the board (REB = 0).
  • Opponent PPP is sensitive to the opponent's transition strength and varies game to game.
  • All data for this project was hand-logged and has been cleaned and double checked, but there is still room for small inconsistencies in data recording that comes from manual collection.
  • This is a five-game sample. The single-game limitation from the earlier study no longer applies, but five games is still a modest sample at the per-level breakdowns.
Designated rebounder: a player whose standing role is to attack the glass (Players C, F, I, and E) IP: in position but made no effort to rebound Free chaser: crashers minus players boxing out, the bodies free to go get the ball REB / OREB% / DREB%: rebounds won divided by rebound opportunities OPP PPP: opponent points per possession on plays where GW fails to secure the rebound. (D) is after a failed defensive rebound, (O) is after a failed offensive rebound.
